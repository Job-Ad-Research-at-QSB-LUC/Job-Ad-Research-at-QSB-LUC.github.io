External validation: shared work and observed mobility

← Occupation navigator · Skills and abilities
Updated 16 October 2026 · manuscript and SI comparisons

What this comparison asks

How well do related-occupation recommendations recover observed occupational moves? Mean recovery averages recovery equally across starting occupations. SST uses career-history transition shares; EMSI uses overlap with its ranked list of twenty destinations. Their percentages answer different questions; compare models within each benchmark.

What this comparison can and cannot establish

Do posting-derived task profiles identify related occupations that also appear in independently sourced records of occupational movement? Observed transitions are one external check on shared work, not a complete measure of opportunity. Hiring practices, credentials, geography, wages, and other skills-related constraints—including required knowledge and abilities—can affect which occupational moves occur. Absence from a transition record is not evidence that a transition is impossible. Similarity is not a transition probability or a claim of readiness for a job.

Tasks are a form of skills data: they describe concrete activities performed at work. Task descriptions, broader skill categories, knowledge, and abilities capture distinct aspects of work requirements. This analysis distinguishes these representations and tests how well each identifies related occupations. Task vectors use the normalized square root of RCA (occupation task share divided by pooled task share), without an RCA cutoff. This page compares task and O*NET representations; it does not use transition frequency to train or weight the navigator's recommendations. Results do not establish better placements or the causal effectiveness of employment services.

Why SST uses 19 rather than 20. The broader SOC6 illustration and the SST evaluation use different candidate populations. Four destinations in the broader published-O*NET top 20 cannot pass our one-to-one SOC2010–SOC2018 crosswalk: Paramedics, Emergency Medical Technicians, Pediatric Surgeons, and Neurologists. Three destinations below that broader top-20 cutoff remain eligible—Psychologists, All Other; Obstetricians and Gynecologists; and Physical Therapists—so the full mapped O*NET ranking supplies 19 distinct destinations. The ranking combines published lists for the five RN specialties; this is not simply deleting one entry from the generic RN occupation's original list.

To match that length, we retain the first 19 posting-task recommendations and drop its rank-20 destination, Respiratory Therapists (29-1126). Respiratory Therapists are still an eligible SST destination and remain in O*NET's list. We remove them only from the posting-task recommendation list for this equal-length comparison—not from observed outcomes or the candidate pool. That changes RN posting-task capture from 27.50% to 27.42%. The rule is based on model ranks and available list lengths, not on whether that last recommendation helps the score.

Why CPS + SIPP uses nine. Its Census occupation groups are broader than SST's SOC occupations. Tracking the 19 eligible SST O*NET destinations into the CPS + SIPP candidate mapping gives:

Mapping step Effect on the 19 destinations
Six physician occupations fall in “Physicians and surgeons,” a Census group excluded by our conservative SOC-vintage crosswalk rule −6
“Psychologists, All Other” and “Psychiatric Technicians” fall in other excluded Census groups with non-one-to-one SOC-vintage mappings −2
“Mental Health Counselors” falls in a broader counselors group not fully represented in the fixed model population −1
“Nursing Assistants” and “Psychiatric Aides” merge into one nursing, psychiatric, and home health aides category −1
Distinct eligible destinations remaining 9

These are classification and candidate-population restrictions, not a low destination-frequency threshold or evidence that those occupations are absent from the surveys. The mapping rule requires unambiguous SOC-vintage correspondences and complete representation of each retained Census group. It is a conservative analysis choice; nine is not an inherent limit of O*NET. We then apply the equal-length rule separately within this benchmark.

For CPS + SIPP, start with 400 eligible candidate destinations and the same 262 observed RN occupation-change records. After occupation mapping, the posting-task ranking supplies 20 recommendations and published O*NET supplies nine. We take the first nine destinations from each ranking, without using transition outcomes to choose them.

For each list, add the supplied survey weights of RN transitions into its recommended destinations, then divide by the weight of all 262 RN changes, including transitions to destinations outside the candidate pool. These percentages are weighted shares, not counts divided by 262.

  • Posting tasks, first 9: capture 27.85% of observed transition weight.
  • Published O*NET, all 9: capture 27.45%.
  • Difference: 0.40 percentage points, compared with 11.49 points under the original 20-versus-9 comparison.

The additional posting-task recommendations at ranks 10–20 capture another 11.09 percentage points. They explain almost all of the larger original RN CPS + SIPP gap. Equal lengths reveal a small descriptive difference here, not evidence of a statistically significant advantage for RN. SST and EMSI retain larger gaps in the table above.

How to read these comparisons

Mean capture / recovery asks how much observed mobility the recommendations recover, averaged equally across origins. Share of origins with more recovery asks how widespread a positive difference is; a tiny positive difference and a large one each count as one origin. Ties stay in the denominator. The two panels complement each other: one shows the average amount recovered, the other the prevalence of greater recovery.

Survey benchmarks: coverage and pooling

Survey detail and pooling

CPS and SIPP are pooled by stacking their records and preserving supplied weights. NLSY97 and PSID are pooled separately for each horizon. This is not an average of separate-source headline percentages. Pooling increases eligible origins but does not resolve crosswalk exclusions, unequal source contributions or repeated observations. Trajectory weights can be dominated by NLSY97; they are not an equal-source consensus.

Methods and limits

Posting profiles retain the corrected research task assignments with a support floor of five sampled job-months and the frozen representation. Similarities are aggregated to benchmark occupation codes using the audited mappings. The original task/DWA crosswalks are labeled O*NET 27.2; the official 2025 relatedness algorithm used O*NET 30.0 inputs, and the published comparator uses released 31.0 lists. Do not interpret this as a synchronized latest-vintage experiment.

CPS/SIPP use Census2010 codes. The trajectories use Census2002, conservatively mapped through accepted unchanged-scope correspondences. Self-transitions are excluded, ties are deterministic, and outcomes outside the candidate pool remain in overall denominators. For example, RN has 262 eligible CPS–SIPP changes (Census2010 3255), but its older Census2002 3130 code is excluded by the audited correspondence, so the three-year focal RN result is unavailable—not zero.

RN's published O*NET list contains 20 detailed occupations, but Census aggregation absorbs four RN specialties into the origin itself. Conservative SOC-vintage crosswalk rules exclude physician and EMT/paramedic groups with non-one-to-one mappings. Combining the lists of all five RN specialties leaves nine eligible Census destinations. This is a mapping restriction, not a low destination-frequency threshold. CPS–SIPP comparisons use Census groups; for example, nursing assistants and orderlies share the broader nursing, psychiatric, and home health aides category. A missing badge can reflect mapping or a rank outside the observed top twenty.

Descriptive intervals condition on fixed profiles, outcomes, weights and crosswalks; they are not full survey-design intervals and omit several sources of measurement uncertainty. Overlapping horizons are not independent evidence. Posting data describe advertised demand, which differs from all work actually performed. Shared tasks can suggest a connection while licensing, training and other requirements still matter.
